# Sparse or restricted sources

Read when access is incomplete, restricted processing matters, or existing cases
could reveal methods. This workflow does not arrange interviews. These options
do not authorize purchases, uploads, contact, or sharing.

| Situation | Useful next move | Preserve this limitation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Paywalled book/course | Authorized edition, lawful preview, author manuscript, or permitted excerpts | Metadata or a few pages cannot stand for the whole work |
| Proprietary reports/notes | Verify permission for the processing environment; approved local route if required | Derived rules and quotations may remain confidential |
| Few publications, recorded talks/interviews exist | Extract specific cases, corrections, and contrasting examples | Interview claims are self-report until compared with behavior |
| Expert unavailable, historical accounts exist | Existing authorized accounts, review comments, or correspondence | Proxy testimony is not direct expert evidence |
| Mostly final outputs | Ask for drafts, rejected alternatives, annotations, or review comments | Several methods may explain the same final output |
| Visual/physical expertise | Authorized demonstrations with observable cues and outcomes | Text may omit sensory and motor information |
| Missing material everywhere | Narrow the task or produce a gap/acquisition plan | Do not invent a corpus from model memory |

An inaccessible source is an inventory entry, not support. A bibliography citation
establishes a work exists, not what it says. Fill task gaps with alternative sources
while preserving their attribution; do not transfer their methods to the target expert.

## Reconstruct decisions instead of slogans

Use an existing authorized account of a concrete incident, preferably with
contemporary artifacts. Reconstruct information available at each decision point
separately from later outcomes. Capture observable rationale, not inaccessible
internal cognition. Search the available sources for answers to the questions below;
leave absent answers unknown. Do not generate an imagined interview.

Adapt these analytical questions to the field:

1. Which documented case shows a changed first impression? What information
   was available at the time, and what happened in what order?
2. Which cue did the expert identify as important? Is there evidence a novice missed it?
3. Which plausible alternative was rejected? What evidence distinguished it?
4. Do similar cases with one changed feature produce different decisions?
5. Was an expected result recorded beforehand? What triggered revision?
6. Which failure or exception limits the apparent rule?
7. Can the draft method explain a reserved case without seeing its outcome?

Pair similar cases with different expert choices to expose discriminating cues.
Include routine, ambiguous, successful, and failed cases. Preserve uncertainty,
chronology, and revisions; distinguish retrospective accounts from contemporary
records. Reserve evaluation cases before extracting rules.

Choose the next source or case that separates competing candidate methods.
Do not collect biography or word count for its own sake. Agree a time/cost budget.

## Evidence examples

- One lecture explicitly says to sketch a limiting case: direct single-source
  support for that teaching method, not a universal personality trait.
- A book repeats the lecture verbatim: more access, no new original context.
- Two cases show boundary checks without a general statement: inferred method,
  with case locators and scope.
- A colleague says the expert always checks boundaries: proxy evidence; "always"
  remains the colleague's assertion.
- A model recalls the expert is famous for first principles: unsupported until a
  relevant source is retrieved. Do not fabricate citations.

## Research basis

The Critical Decision Method probes incidents for situation assessment, critical
cues, and decision strategies. The source-analysis questions here are an adaptation
of that idea for existing records; they are not an interview protocol or a validated
substitute for interviewing an expert. See Klein and MacGregor,
*Knowledge Elicitation of Recognition-Primed Decision Making* (1988),
[author-uploaded report and abstract](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/235199994_Knowledge_Elicitation_of_Recognition-Primed_Decision_Making).
Related paper: Klein, Calderwood, and MacGregor (1989),
[Critical decision method for eliciting knowledge](https://doi.org/10.1109/21.31053).
